# The physics of corona26

Everything this project computes, written down before any of it is implemented.
Each section states the equations, the approximation being made, and what
breaks if the approximation is wrong.

Notation: `R☉ = 6.957e8 m`, `r` is heliocentric distance, `(θ, φ)` are
colatitude and longitude in a heliographic frame, `ne` is electron number
density, `B` is magnetic flux density.

---

## 1. The problem

During totality the photospheric disk is occulted and what remains visible is
the corona: a 10⁻⁶-of-the-disk structure whose shape is set by the coronal
magnetic field. The visible-light corona has three components:

| Component | Source | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **K-corona** | Thomson scattering of photospheric light off *free electrons* | Strongly polarised, continuum, dominant below ~2 R☉ |
| **F-corona** | Diffraction/scattering off *interplanetary dust* | Weakly polarised, Fraunhofer lines survive, dominant above ~2.5 R☉ |
| **E-corona** | Coronal emission lines (Fe XIV 5303 Å etc.) | Narrow, small contribution to broadband |

**We model the K-corona only.** It is the component that carries the magnetic
structure, and the one that makes streamers visible. The F-corona is a smooth,
almost featureless background that would add brightness without adding shape.

This has a direct consequence for validation: comparing our synthetic image
against a raw eclipse photograph is comparing K against K+F+E. Either we
compare against a K-corona-separated product, or we compare *shape* (angular
structure at fixed radius) rather than absolute brightness. We do the latter.

---

## 2. The chain

```
Br(θ, φ)  photospheric boundary  →  §3
    ↓ PFSS: ∇²Φ = 0                  §4
B(r, θ, φ)  3-D coronal field
    ↓ field-line integration          §5
open/closed topology
    ↓ empirical proxy                 §6
ne(r, θ, φ)
    ↓ Thomson scattering + LOS        §7
I(x, y)  synthetic K-corona
    ↓ observer geometry               §8
image as seen from Colmenar Viejo
```

The honest one-line description of the whole thing:

> This is not a thermodynamic MHD model. It is a PFSS reconstruction plus a
> topology-informed electron-density proxy, rendered through an exact Thomson
> scattering integral.

The word *exact* belongs only to §7. Sections 4 and 6 are approximations, and
§6 is frankly a fit.

## 3. Boundary condition: the photospheric magnetic field

PFSS needs `Br` over the **whole** sphere. We can only see one hemisphere from
Earth. That gap is the single largest source of error in this project — larger
than the PFSS assumption, larger than the density proxy.

### Sources

**ADAPT-GONG** (primary). ADAPT (Air Force Data Assimilative Photospheric flux
Transport) takes GONG magnetograms and evolves them with a surface flux
transport model — differential rotation, meridional flow, supergranular
diffusion — to estimate the far side. Each ADAPT file contains **12
realisations** generated by varying the model's assumptions.

Those 12 realisations are not a nuisance. They are a free, physically motivated
**uncertainty ensemble on the boundary condition**, and using them is one of
the more scientifically defensible things this project does.

**GONG synoptic** (fallback). A Carrington rotation map — a mosaic of central
meridian strips over ~27 days. Cheaper, but the far side is *old data* rather
than a transport estimate, which at solar maximum is worse.

Access is through `sunpy.net.Fido` with `ADAPTClient` / `GONGClient`
(both verified present in sunpy 8.0.0).

### Why this dominates the error budget

August 2026 is near solar maximum. Active regions emerge and evolve on
timescales of days. A region that emerged on the far side four days ago is,
in a flux-transport map, an *extrapolation*, not an observation. If it is
large enough to anchor a helmet streamer, our predicted streamer belt will be
wrong in a way no amount of renderer quality can fix.

Predictive Science mitigate exactly this by assimilating **Solar Orbiter/PHI
far-side magnetograms**. We cannot; that is a real, statable advantage they
have over us, and it goes in the README.

## 4. Coronal field: potential field source surface (PFSS)

### Derivation

In the low corona the plasma beta is small, so we assume the field is
approximately force-free, and then take the strongest version of that: current-free.

```
∇ × B = 0
```

A curl-free field is a gradient of a scalar potential:

```
B = -∇Φ
```

Combining with the solenoidal constraint `∇ · B = 0`:

```
∇²Φ = 0
```

Laplace's equation. In spherical coordinates the general solution separates
into spherical harmonics:

```
Φ(r, θ, φ) = Σ_l Σ_m [ a_lm r^l + b_lm r^(-l-1) ] Y_lm(θ, φ)
```

### Boundary conditions

Lower boundary — match the observed radial field at the photosphere:

```
Br(R☉, θ, φ) = magnetogram(θ, φ)
```

Upper boundary — the **source surface** at `r = Rss`, where the field is forced
purely radial:

```
Bθ(Rss, θ, φ) = 0
Bφ(Rss, θ, φ) = 0
```

This is the model's physical content: it is a stand-in for the solar wind
dragging the field open. Beyond `Rss` the field is assumed radial and the
model says nothing.

### What PFSS gets wrong, on purpose

- **No currents.** Real coronal fields carry currents; sheared and twisted
  active-region fields are not potential. PFSS gives the lowest-energy field
  consistent with the boundary, so it systematically *under*-shears.
- **No plasma.** No density, no temperature, no pressure, no force balance.
  This is why §6 exists at all.
- **The source surface is fictional.** There is no physical spherical surface
  where the field becomes radial. It is a boundary condition chosen to mimic
  the effect of the wind.
- **It is static.** No time evolution, no CMEs.

### The Rss ensemble

`Rss = 2.5 R☉` is the historical default (Altschuler & Newkirk 1969). It is
convention, not measurement, and recent work shows the value that best
reproduces observed coronal structure varies with the solar cycle — often
*lower* than 2.5 near maximum.

Benavitz, Boe & Habbal (2024) used eclipse white-light images as the benchmark
for exactly this question, which makes it the closest published precedent to
what we are doing.

So we do not pick one. We run:

```
Rss ∈ {1.3, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0} R☉
```

Combined with the 12 ADAPT realisations that is a **60-member ensemble**, and
the spread across it is our honest error bar. Lower `Rss` opens more flux and
produces more, narrower streamers; higher `Rss` produces fewer, broader ones.
Publishing that spread is more useful than publishing one confident image.

### Implementation

`sunkit-magex` (the maintained successor to `pfsspy`) is the V1 engine.
Verified API:

```python
from sunkit_magex import pfss
inp = pfss.Input(br_map, nr, rss)     # nr = radial grid points
out = pfss.pfss(inp)
```

A hand-written spherical-harmonic solver in JAX is planned for **V1.1, after
the eclipse**, validated *against* sunkit-magex. Writing it first would be the
single most likely way to miss the deadline, and validating an own
implementation against an established one is a better portfolio story than
writing one and hoping.

## 5. Magnetic topology

Trace field lines by integrating

```
dx/ds = B(x) / |B(x)|
```

from seed points, and classify:

- **Closed** — both footpoints return to `r = R☉`. Traps plasma. Overdense.
  These make **streamers** and **helmet streamers**.
- **Open** — reaches `r = Rss`. Plasma escapes as solar wind. Underdense.
  These make **coronal holes**, dark in white light.

The boundary between the two — the separatrix web, and the **heliospheric
current sheet** where the open field changes polarity — is where the
**streamer belt** lives, and it is the brightest large-scale structure in an
eclipse image.

`sunkit-magex` gives us this directly: `FieldLine.is_open`, `.polarity`,
`.expansion_factor`, `.solar_footpoint`, `.source_surface_footpoint`
(all verified present).

`expansion_factor` — the flux-tube expansion factor `f_s` of Wang & Sheeley —
is worth keeping: it is the classic predictor of solar wind speed, and speed
anticorrelates with density. It is a better density modulator than a binary
open/closed flag, and it costs us nothing because the library already computes it.

## 6. Electron density: the honest weak point

PFSS gives no plasma. To render anything we must invent `ne`. This section is
a **proxy**, is labelled as such everywhere, and its parameters are knobs, not
physics.

### Radial profile

Empirical fits to eclipse and coronagraph observations (Baumbach 1937;
Allen 1947; Saito, Poland & Munro 1977) take the form

```
n0(r) = A r⁻² + B r⁻⁴ + C r⁻⁶       [r in R☉]
```

Physical reading of the terms:

- `r⁻²` — asymptotic solar wind. Mass conservation in a radial flow at roughly
  constant speed gives `ne ∝ r⁻²`. Dominates far out.
- `r⁻⁶`, `r⁻⁴` — the steep near-Sun falloff of gravitationally stratified,
  closed-field plasma. Dominate below ~1.5 R☉.

The Baumbach–Allen coefficients (~`1.55e8 r⁻⁶ + 2.99e8 r⁻¹⁶` in some forms,
`1e8`-scale in cm⁻³) are the starting point; we fit our own normalisation so
that the ensemble mean matches a published K-corona radial brightness profile.

Note the enormous dynamic range: between 1.1 and 3 R☉ brightness falls by
~4 orders of magnitude. Any visualisation must be log-scaled or filtered, which
is exactly why every published eclipse-prediction image uses a radial filter.

### Topological modulation

```
ne(r, θ, φ) = n0(r) · f(topology)
```

with, as a first pass:

```
closed field  →  f ∈ [3, 6]      (streamers, overdense)
open field    →  f ∈ [0.5, 1]    (coronal holes, underdense)
```

A smooth version, preferred over the binary switch because sharp edges render
as unphysical discontinuities:

```
f = f_open + (f_closed - f_open) · σ(closed-ness)
```

where closed-ness can be built from distance to the heliospheric current sheet
or from `1/expansion_factor`.

### What this cannot do

It cannot produce anything the magnetic topology does not already contain.
No thermal structure, no wave heating, no CMEs, no dynamics. If the corona on
12 August contains a structure not implied by the PFSS topology, we will miss
it entirely. That is a prediction, and it is falsifiable tomorrow.

## 7. The renderer: Thomson scattering

This is the part that is genuinely exact, and the part we write ourselves.

### The mechanism

A free electron in the coronal plasma is accelerated by the electric field of
photospheric light and re-radiates. Classical, elastic, wavelength-independent,
and — critically — **the corona is optically thin**: `τ ≈ 10⁻⁶`, so single
scattering is not an approximation but an excellent description. No multiple
scattering, no radiative transfer solve. A pure line-of-sight integral.

(Contrast with `snow-mcrt`, where `τ` is enormous and multiple scattering is
the entire problem. Same physics of scattering, opposite optical-depth regime.
Worth stating explicitly in the write-up.)

### Cross-section

```
σ_T = (8π/3) r_e² ≈ 6.65e-29 m²
r_e = e²/(4πε₀ m_e c²) ≈ 2.818e-15 m
```

### Geometry

Two angles matter:

- **χ** — the scattering angle between the incoming photospheric ray and the
  line of sight.
- **Ω** — the half-angle subtended by the Sun at the scattering electron:

```
sin Ω = R☉ / r
```

`Ω` is what makes this non-trivial. The Sun is **not a point source**. An
electron at 1.5 R☉ sees a solar disk 42° wide, so the scattering angle varies
across the disk and must be integrated over it. Treating the Sun as a point
source is the classic beginner error here and gets both the brightness and the
polarisation wrong.

### Van de Hulst / Billings coefficients

Integrating over the finite, limb-darkened disk with limb darkening

```
L(cos ζ) = L☉ (1 - u + u cos ζ),    u ≈ 0.6 in visible light
```

gives four functions of `Ω` alone — `A(Ω)`, `B(Ω)`, `C(Ω)`, `D(Ω)`
(van de Hulst 1950; Minnaert 1930; popularised by Billings 1966). They have
closed forms in `sin Ω` and `cos Ω`, so they are **cheap: one evaluation per
sample point, no nested disk integral at runtime**. This is what makes a
GPU render of hundreds of millions of samples tractable.

The scattered intensities per electron, tangential (perpendicular to the
scattering plane) and radial (in-plane):

```
I_tan(r)  = (π σ_e L☉ / 2) [ (1-u) C(Ω) + u D(Ω) ]
I_rad(r)  = (π σ_e L☉ / 2) { [ (1-u) C(Ω) + u D(Ω) ]
                           - [ (1-u) A(Ω) + u B(Ω) ] sin²χ }
```

The observables:

```
total brightness      B  = I_tan + I_rad
polarised brightness  pB = I_tan - I_rad
```

`pB` is the more physically clean quantity — it isolates the K-corona, because
the F-corona is nearly unpolarised. PSI publish `pB` as well as total
brightness, so **rendering both gives us a cleaner comparison channel**.

### Line-of-sight integral

For each pixel:

```
I(x, y) = ∫_LOS  ne(r(l)) · K(r(l), χ(l))  dl
```

where `K` is whichever of the kernels above we want. Discretised as a simple
quadrature along each ray — no adaptive stepping in V1, just enough samples
that the result is converged, checked by doubling.

Note there is **no `exp(-τ)` term**. Optically thin. That absence is the whole
reason this is a one-pass integral and not a Monte Carlo.

### Why this is the GPU part

A 1024×1024 image with ~512 samples per ray is ~5×10⁸ independent kernel
evaluations, each needing an `ne` lookup and a handful of transcendentals.
Every sample is independent of every other — a pure map-reduce. This is the
one place in the pipeline where a GPU is obviously the right tool, and it is
where JAX `vmap` + `jit` earn their place. Rendered in tiles to bound VRAM.

## 8. Observer geometry: what we will actually see from Colmenar Viejo

A 3-D corona is not an image. It must be projected from the real observer's
position and rotated to the real sky orientation.

Computed for **2026-08-12 18:31 UTC (20:31 CEST)** at Colmenar Viejo
(40.659° N, 3.768° W, 1004 m), using astropy/sunpy — these are measured
outputs, not quoted:

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| `P` — solar north position angle | **15.19°** |
| `B0` — heliographic latitude of disk centre | **6.51°** |
| `L0` — Carrington longitude of disk centre | **224.53°** |
| Solar angular radius | **15.78′** |
| Sun altitude | **7.55°** |
| Sun azimuth | **283.04°** (WNW) |

Two things follow.

**For the render.** `B0 = 6.51°` means we are looking 6.5° above the solar
equator, so the southern polar hole is foreshortened and the northern one is
better exposed. `P = 15.19°` sets the rotation of the whole image on the sky.
An image with solar north up is *not* what a camera on a tripod will record.
We produce both: north-up (comparable with PSI's left panel) and
horizon-referenced (what the eye sees).

**For the observation.** The Sun will be **7.55° above the horizon at azimuth
283°**. That is very low. Consequences: strong atmospheric extinction and
reddening, a long airmass, and a hard requirement for an unobstructed WNW
horizon. From Colmenar Viejo the Sierra de Guadarrama lies in roughly that
direction — the observing site has to be chosen against a horizon profile,
not a map.

Low altitude also means the corona will appear against a brighter twilight
background than in a high-Sun eclipse, which compresses the visible dynamic
range. Our synthetic image should be filtered to a comparable range before
any visual comparison is claimed.

## 9. Validation

Three-way, in increasing order of honesty:

1. **Against PSI/MAS.** Predictive Science are running a time-dependent MHD
   prediction for this eclipse, updated in near-real-time, with HMI vector
   magnetograms and Solar Orbiter/PHI far-side assimilation. They publish a
   view oriented for Palencia, Spain. They are the professional benchmark and
   they will be better than us; the interesting question is *where* and *by
   how much*.
2. **Against the real corona.** The Sun itself, tomorrow. There is no appeal
   against this dataset.
3. **Against ourselves.** The 60-member ensemble spread tells us which
   features we actually predicted and which are artefacts of one parameter
   choice. A streamer that survives all 60 members is a prediction. One that
   appears in three is noise.

### Metrics

Beyond "they look alike":

- **Streamer position angle.** Extract angular intensity profiles on rings at
  `r = 1.5, 2.0, 2.5 R☉`, find peaks, compare peak angles in degrees. This is
  the primary metric — it is what a coronal physicist would actually check.
- **Angular correlation** of those profiles.
- **IoU** of binarised bright regions after radial filtering.
- **SSIM** as a secondary, visual-only metric. It is not a physics metric and
  will not be reported as one.

All comparisons are made after applying the *same* radial filter and the *same*
angular scale to all three images. Comparing differently-processed images is
the easiest way to produce a meaningless figure.

## 10. Summary of every approximation

| # | Approximation | Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Far-side field is modelled, not observed | **Highest** | 12-member ADAPT ensemble; stated in README |
| 2 | Current-free corona (PFSS) | High | 5-value `Rss` ensemble; compare against MHD |
| 3 | Source surface is fictional | High | Same ensemble |
| 4 | Static field, no evolution | Medium | Use the latest map available before totality |
| 5 | Density is an empirical proxy | **High** | Labelled everywhere; parameters published |
| 6 | K-corona only, no F or E | Medium | Compare shape, not absolute brightness |
| 7 | Single scattering | Negligible | `τ ≈ 10⁻⁶`; genuinely exact |
| 8 | Finite-sample LOS quadrature | Low | Convergence check by sample doubling |

Rows 7 and 8 are the only ones we control tightly. Rows 1 and 5 are where the
prediction will fail, and saying so in advance is the point.
